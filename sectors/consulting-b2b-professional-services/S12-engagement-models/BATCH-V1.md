## The Section Role

How the firm can be hired, and what each arrangement does to its own behaviour.

## The Trap

The default for this section role is a three-tier pricing table: Bronze / Silver / Gold, a tick
matrix of features, and a *most popular* badge on the middle column. Every part of that is
unavailable here, and for three separate reasons:

- **A tick matrix is a table**, which `../CONSULTING-THEME-CONTRACT.md` rules out as a default
  device along with the rest of the document idiom.
- **Tiers imply that more money buys a better firm.** In consulting that is false — the same people
  do the work under every arrangement — so a tier ladder is a claim the firm cannot make.
- **The contract omits currency figures entirely.** There is no price on this page, in any study.

So the section has to be useful without a number, a tier or a comparison grid.

## The Governing Idea

> **Every fee model pays us to do something. Read the one that pays us to do the wrong thing.**

A fixed fee pays the firm to finish early. Time and materials pays it to take longer. A retainer pays
it to still be here. A secondment pays it to be agreeable. None of those incentives is hidden or
shameful — they are arithmetic — but a buyer is almost never shown them, and **the misalignment is
usually what decides how the engagement ends.**

So every model on this page carries its own misaligned incentive, written by the firm about itself,
next to the situation it actually suits.

## The Four Models

Shared across all five studies, so the batch varies by composition rather than by copy.

| Model | Suits | Pays us to |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Fixed fee** | A question whose shape is already known | **Finish early.** If the question changes in week three, the scope becomes the argument |
| **Time and materials** | A problem nobody has framed yet | **Take longer.** The defence is a weekly stop-or-continue, written into the contract |
| **Retainer** | A decision that returns every quarter | **Still be here.** The hardest of these to cancel, so it is capped at `six` months and re-signed |
| **Secondment** | Wanting the capability rather than the answer | **Be agreeable.** Someone inside your organisation stops being able to say the unwelcome thing |

Each defence is a real mechanism rather than a reassurance: the weekly stop-or-continue is the same
exit `S05` sells, and the retainer cap is a policy with a date on it.

## The Declined Model

Every study carries it, and it is the fifth item in the section's argument rather than a footnote.

> **Outcome-based fees. We do not offer them.**
>
> Paying on outcome requires knowing what would have happened otherwise, and nobody runs the company
> twice. A fee resting on a number neither of us can produce is a dispute with a date on it.

Outcome-based pricing is the most requested commercial model in this sector, so refusing it costs
something. The reason is not caution — **it is the position `S08` already took about the firm's own
metrics, applied to the firm's own fees.** The metrics section's epistemics decide the commercial
section's terms.

That is the thing worth protecting across the assembled page: it is what a site looks like when it is
written by one mind rather than by five departments.

## What `003` Found By Sorting

The other four studies list the models. `003` sorts them, and the key is the one a buyer actually
needs: **who absorbs it when the work takes longer than anyone expected.**

The result is deliberately lopsided — **one model puts that risk on the firm, three put it on the
client** — and the study states the asymmetry in words rather than letting the block sizes imply it.
Then it draws the conclusion that makes the sorting worth doing:

> The one model that carries risk for the firm is the one that only works when the question is
> already clear. **The safest model is available on the easiest problems.**

A balanced-looking grid would have been more comfortable to publish and would have hidden that.

## The Conversion Insight, Which `004` Is Built On

The section opens on the thing the firm **will not sell**. The declined model is the first block on
the page, in the accent, at the largest size in the study, and the action turns the same move into an
offer: *tell us the situation and we will say which of these we would refuse to sell you.*

**A seller who names what they will not sell has told you something about every other thing on the
page.** It converts by narrowing rather than widening, which is the opposite of what a commercial
section usually does, and it is credible precisely because it costs the firm a sale.

Five consecutive sections, five conversion bases, none repeating another:

| Study | Converts on |
| --- | --- |
| `S08-004` | **Audit** — ask about the number we would rather not print |
| `S09-004` | **Verification** — quotes sorted by who will take a call |
| `S10-004` | **Relevance** — the wall cannot tell you whether we have met your problem |
| `S11-004` | **Giving the method away** — take these questions into your own meeting |
| `S12-004` | **Declining the sale** — the model we will not sell you, first |

This matters most at this variant, because these are the five blocks a buyer scanning the assembled
page for commercial terms will actually stop on.

## The Media Question

A fee model has nothing to photograph, and a photograph of a contract would be the document metaphor
the contract bans. But this section does describe one recurring physical thing:

> **The weekly meeting where the engagement is stopped or continued.**

It is the mechanism the models keep referring to — the defence against the time-and-materials
incentive, and the same exit `S05` sells. Three studies carry it; two carry none.

## Notes Per Study

**`001`** — the safe reading. Four equal cards, each split so the incentive sits in a tinted strip at
the foot, which puts the four incentives on one horizontal line the eye can read across. The refusal
closes the section **and carries the action**, so the study ends on one block rather than two stacked
panels.

**`002`** — **shape C.** No cards, no tiers, no grid. Each model is a name at standfirst size, the
situation in the reading face, and the incentive as its own accent-ruled closing line. The incentive
is always last in the block, because it is what a reader should leave with and what every other firm
leaves out.

**`003`** — **the sort.** See above.

**`004`** — **the refusal leads.** See above.

**`005`** — **the small print is the headline.** *Finish early. Take longer. Still be here. Be
agreeable.* are set at display size in the accent, and the model names sit beneath them as captions.
A reader scanning the page takes away a list of the firm's own temptations rather than a list of
products — and it removes the last resemblance to a pricing table, where the product name is always
the largest thing in the cell. Nothing is ranked or badged, because the point is that all four are
compromised in different directions.

## Two Corrections Made During Authoring

**`CONS-S12-001` collided with `CONS-S09-001`.** The check reported an 89% sequence match —
`HEAD2>LIST4>PANEL>OFFER` against `HEAD2>LIST4>PANEL>DECLIN>OFFER`. Both were a head, a four-item
row, a panel and an accent offer block. Moving the reserved area did not help, because the checker
classifies a band as a panel and the token sequence was unchanged.

The fix was structural rather than cosmetic: **the action was folded into the refusal block** and the
separate offer panel removed. The study now ends on one block that both declines outcome-based fees
and asks for the situation, which is a tighter ending than two stacked panels and drops the match to
75%.

**A CSS selector was too broad in `001`.** `.pays span { display: block }` also matched the nested
`<span data-placeholder="true">six</span>`, so the retainer card rendered *"cap it at / six /
months"* across three lines and pushed that card's incentive strip out of alignment with the other
three. Scoped to `.pays > span`. Caught by rendering, not by reading — the markup was correct and
only the cascade was wrong.
