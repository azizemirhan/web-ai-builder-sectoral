## The Section Role

One piece of editorial content in full, read without obstruction.

## The Trap

`S13` named this the most content-farm-shaped section on any professional-services site. The detail
page inherits all of it: a stock lead image, a reading-time badge, a share counter and a newsletter
box wrapped around a piece nobody will finish.

The deeper failure is quieter. **An article carries no expiry date, so every reader arrives assuming
it is current.** A search result from five years ago looks exactly like one from last week. The firm
knows which of its pieces it no longer believes; the reader does not.

## The Governing Idea

> **An article carries no expiry date, so every reader arrives assuming it is current.**

So `S13`'s status field becomes the top of the page. **The note comes before the standfirst**, not
after the body where a correction is conventionally buried, and the piece is unchanged underneath it.
`S13`'s policy holds and every study closes on it:

> **Nothing is taken down. A piece we no longer believe keeps its address and gets this note.**

## What The Section Refuses, And Why It Matters Here

- **No reading time, no view count, no share counter.** `S13` ruled these out as retention metrics
  dressed as courtesy, and a detail page is where they are hardest to resist. The publication context
  instead carries what a reader checking currency actually needs: when it was published, when it was
  last reviewed, and who wrote it.
- **No newsletter box, no gated download.** `S14` established that a gate is a price; `S18` that a
  form is a way of not giving you an address. Both hold here.
- **No lead image.** `S13` named the three photographs this section reaches for — abstract nodes, a
  city at dusk, a laptop and a coffee — all sector anti-patterns. An article about who owns a standard
  has no honest photograph. The only reserved area in the batch is **the person who wrote it**, in
  `001` and `003`, and it sits with the attribution rather than at the top as a lead.

## The Five Compositions

- **`001`** — the safe article page: the note as a filled block between the title and the standfirst,
  publication context on a rule, the body at a controlled measure, related reading and the author at
  the foot.
- **`002`** — a retraction is conventionally set small, in italic, in a tinted box, after the body.
  Every one of those decisions is a way of hoping the reader misses it. Here the note is **the same
  size as the standfirst and directly above it**, in the same face and weight, so the two read as one
  opening statement: *we now think this was wrong*, then the sentence that was wrong.
- **`003`** — the note says the firm was wrong; the obvious next question is **when it found out**, and
  every other treatment leaves that unanswerable. The rail carries the full history: published,
  reviewed and held, reviewed and retracted. **A single correction is an event; a review history is a
  practice**, and only the second is evidence that the other pieces have been looked at too.
- **`004`** — the three conventional conversions are all unavailable, and that is the useful
  constraint: **what is left is what the page actually owes somebody.** The piece was published, read
  and acted on by people the firm will never know about, and it is now retracted. So the panel says
  *if you acted on this, tell us*, and offers what the firm would do now. **An ask that only exists
  because the firm was wrong cannot be written by a firm that is never wrong** — and it is worth
  nothing on the four pieces that still hold.
- **`005`** — **the piece is annotated rather than corrected.** The other four put the retraction at
  the top and leave the body alone, which is honest but blunt: the reader is told the piece is wrong
  and then reads three paragraphs with no idea which part failed. Here the body is still unedited and
  the firm in the present writes in the margin beside it, at the three points where it now disagrees
  with itself. **The reader gets the 2019 argument and the 2024 objection in the same eye movement.**

A margin is the only place a second voice can go without becoming an edit: set the objection inline
and the piece has been rewritten; set it at the bottom and it has been buried. That is why `005` is
the sector-native reading — a firm that sells judgement is selling the ability to change its mind on
evidence, and this is what `S11`'s *what would change your mind?* looks like kept in public, against
its own published work, beside the sentence rather than after it.

### Second tooling fix, and what is left

Last batch fixed the collision checker's shell regex so page-level studies fingerprint at all. This
batch found the next layer: studies that wrap their whole body in a single structural wrapper
(`div class="col"`, `div class="page"`) produced a degenerate one- or two-token sequence, and the
scorer read two degenerate sequences as a 100% match. Three such false positives were reported against
`S25` on the first run. The scanner now **descends one indentation level when the top level yields
fewer than three blocks**; studies with a real top-level sequence are untouched. Sector-wide near
matches fell from **21 to 14**, and no exact collisions remain.

One case is still degenerate and is recorded rather than chased: `S23-003` and `S25-003` both wrap
their body in `div class="page"` whose children are an unclassed `<div>` and an `<aside>`, neither of
which the block regex matches, so both fingerprint as `CTX>PAGE`. They were compared by eye — a
service page with a rail of costs, limits and fee, against an article with a rail of review history —
and share no skeleton. Fixing it means teaching the scanner about `aside` and unclassed wrappers,
which would re-fingerprint the whole sector; not worth doing mid-batch.
