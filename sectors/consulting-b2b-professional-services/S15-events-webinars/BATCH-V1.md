## The Section Role

What the firm does in person, and who else is in the room.

## The Trap

An events section defaults to a grid of cards carrying a stock photograph of a conference stage, a
countdown, *limited seats*, and a webinar that is a sales call with slides. The reader knows this.
The tell is that a normal events grid gives a session for twelve and a conference for four hundred
the same 16:9 photograph, so **there is no way to tell them apart before attending.**

## The Governing Idea

> **What makes an hour worth it is who else is in the room.**

Not the speaker. A session with twelve people who have the same problem is worth travelling for; a
broadcast with five hundred anonymous attendees is a broadcast, however good the slides are.

So every entry names **its format, who it is for, and what its cap is** — and the section says which
of its own events is a broadcast rather than pretending all four are workshops.

## The Four Entries

Shared across all five studies, so the batch varies by composition rather than by copy.

| Format | Event | For | The cap, or what stands in for one |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Room of `twelve`** | The stage-03 session | People whose programme is late and whose reporting says it is fine | Twelve, and we stop there. When it fills we run it again within the quarter |
| **Broadcast** | The counterfactual hour | Anyone. There is no room, so there is no cap | We will not call it a workshop. Recorded, and it goes up whether or not it went well |
| **Someone else's stage** | A conference talk | Whoever bought a ticket. We do not control the room | Useful for deciding whether to talk to us, not for getting work done |
| **Closed** | A client briefing | One client's team. Not open and not on sale | Listed because leaving it off would make the calendar look busier than it is |

## The Cap, And Why It Is Not Scarcity

Twelve is a real limit with a reason: **thirteen makes it a lecture.**

But a real cap is the opposite of a countdown, so every study states what happens when it fills —
**we run it again** — and none carries a timer, a seats-remaining counter or a waiting list that is
really a mailing list.

> **The cap is real. The urgency is not.**

## The Closed Event, Listed Anyway

The fourth entry is a briefing nobody can attend. Leaving it off would make the calendar look busier
with *open* events than it is.

An events section is a claim about how much is happening, and that claim is only checkable if the
unavailable things are on it too. In `003` it carries the same display figure as the rest — *not
you* — at the same size, so the arithmetic of the page stays honest rather than being softened into
a footnote.

## Why There Are No Speakers

Most events sections sell the speaker, with a headshot and a title. This one sells the room.

Portraits stay reserved sector-wide in any case, but the omission here is **a position rather than a
constraint**: the speaker is the least reliable predictor of whether an hour was worth it, and every
reader who has sat through a good speaker in a bad room already knows that.

## The Media Question

The honest image is **the room, at the size it is actually run.** It is the rare case where the
reserved area *is* the argument: a photograph of twelve chairs cannot be mistaken for an auditorium.

`002` takes this furthest by shaping each reserved area like its room — the capped session nearly
square, the broadcast a wide flat strip because there is no room at all, the conference hall
panoramic, the closed briefing the smallest area on the page. **A reader who never gets past the
images has still been told the truth.**

`003` carries none, because a photograph of a room would soften the figure rather than support it.

## The Conversion Insight, Which `004` Is Built On

An events section converts by making every entry look worth attending. This one **disqualifies three
of its own four.**

The broadcast is called a broadcast. The conference talk is described as useful for deciding whether
to talk to the firm and not for getting work done. The client briefing is closed. Only the capped
session gets the room image, the full width and the action.

> A calendar where everything is recommended is a calendar nobody trusts.

**This is not the same move as `S12-004`.** That study declines to sell a commercial model it does
not offer; this one discounts three things it *does* offer, on the record, in favour of a fourth. One
is a refusal, this is a ranking — and it costs three registrations to earn one attendance worth
something.

## Notes Per Study

**`001`** — the safe reading. One image of the room at real size, then the four events across a row,
each with its format pill, audience and cap, closing on the anti-urgency panel.

**`002`** — **shape A, and the only grid in the batch.** The reserved areas are shaped like the
rooms, and the right column is dropped half a block so no two ever sit level — the *offset gallery*
device earning its place, because aligned rows would invite exactly the comparison the differing
ratios exist to prevent.

**`003`** — **the share.** Every module leads with *1 of 12*, *1 of many*, *1 of a few hundred*, *not
you*. One field does the work of a paragraph, and no events section in this sector prints it because
three of the four figures are unflattering.

**`004`** — **the disqualification.** See above.

**`005`** — **column width runs inversely to audience size.** The session for twelve takes roughly
half the band and the others narrow away to the right. It is a division **across** the page rather
than a cascade **down** it — `S09-005` descends by type scale, `S11-005` by indent, `S13-005` by
flagship position; here all four sit on the same top line at once, so the proportions are compared
rather than sequenced. A cascade asks the reader to remember; a division lets them measure.

## Two Corrections, And A Pattern Now Worth Naming

**`005` lost content to its own composition.** Drafted at 148 words, because the three narrow columns
had been given short sentences to fit their width — so the study carried less information than the
other four about the same three events. The columns were widened and the full fields restored.

**This is the third consecutive art-directed variant to do it.** `S13-005` was drafted with four
pieces where the others carried five; `S14-005` was drafted without the cost lines on its two gated
reports; `S15-005` compressed the text of three columns.

The pattern is specific and now worth stating: **when the art-directed study gives one item a
dominant position, the demoted items lose words rather than only size.** The hierarchy is supposed to
be typographic; three times running it became informational. Worth checking first in every future
`005`.

**The room image in `005` broke its own claim.** The header said all four items sit on the same
baseline so the proportions can be compared — but the image sat above the text in column one and
pushed its content halfway down the page, so nothing aligned. Moved below the text; all four columns
now start on the same line, which is what the composition was arguing for.

**A class name confused the collision checker again.** The four-column list in `005` was called
`band`, which the checker matches as a panel before it tests whether the element is a `ul` — the same
failure as `claims` in `S13-004`. Renamed to `spread`; the fingerprint is now the accurate
`HEAD1>LEAD>LIST4>POLICY`. Two occurrences in three batches suggests the checker's ordering is worth
fixing rather than working around, and that is now an open item.
