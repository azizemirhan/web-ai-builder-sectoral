What we change, and by when

The large decisions were made before we were in the room.

The frame, the substructure, the orientation, the glazing and whether anything on the site is kept — all of it drawn by somebody else, and between them they hold most of a building’s carbon. What follows is the part that is ours, with the point at which each of them closes.

There is a version of this page that applies an adjective to this firm, prints a figure nobody outside it has checked, and does not mention a single date anywhere.

This is the other one. Every entry below names a thing we actually do, the point in the programme after which we can no longer do it, and the claim it does not entitle us to make — that last one in the margin, because the qualification should never be further from the claim than a glance.

A note on the margin

Everything set out here is a limit on what the paragraph beside it means. An adjective is not a mechanism, and a mechanism does not extend past what it mechanically does.

  1. Segregation on site, rather than a mixed skip

    Timber, metal, plasterboard and inert kept apart, each in its own container, so that what leaves the gate leaves as a material rather than as a load of mixed rubbish.

    The window

    Compound layout. Once the gate and the standing are where they are, another stream stops being a bin and becomes a lorry movement — and a full skip is moved whether it was sorted or not.

    What it does not entitle us to say

    That it makes the building anything at all. It keeps plasterboard out of a mixed load, which is the expensive problem and the smelly one.

  2. Reuse before recycling, on the same site

    Brick, steel, slate, joinery — whether a material stays where it is and remains the thing it was, or leaves on a lorry and comes back as aggregate.

    The window

    Before the machine arrives. Once a structure is down you have rubble, and rubble is recycling at best. It is the shortest window on this page and the one most often missed, usually by a fortnight.

    What it does not entitle us to say

    That crushing on site is reuse. It is recycling done nearer, and we will call it that. Reuse means the thing is still the thing.

  3. The substitutions we can put forward

    A handful of build-ups where an alternative does the same job for less. The frame and the substructure are not among them, and they are where the weight sits, which is the honest shape of a contractor’s influence.

    The window

    Between appointment and the frame order. Afterwards a substitution is a variation, and a variation costs more than the substitution saves.

    What it does not entitle us to say

    That we designed the building. We proposed against a drawing and somebody accepted or refused, and which it was is in the correspondence.

  4. What the site runs on while it is running

    Whether the compound draws from a supply or from a generator, and what the plant burns while it is standing as well as while it is working — which is the half of it nobody counts.

    The window

    The temporary services application, before mobilisation. Miss it and the site runs on a generator for the duration of the job, and nothing said about it afterwards changes that.

    What it does not entitle us to say

    That a plant list is a carbon figure. It is a list of machines; the fuel records against it are the only part anybody could check.

  5. Deliveries, dust, and what leaves in the water

    How many vehicles come through somebody else’s street and when, and whether what runs off the site is treated before it goes anywhere.

    The window

    The logistics plan, written with the programme rather than after it. Consolidation has to be designed into the sequence, or it becomes lorries waiting on a road with their engines going.

    What it does not entitle us to say

    That a delivery plan is an environmental policy. It is a traffic decision that happens also to be an environmental one, and the people it affects live there.

  6. What is measured, and by whom

    On its own, nothing — worth saying plainly, because measurement is the part of this subject most often presented as though it were an achievement.

    The window

    Continuous, which is the difficulty. A record kept from the first week is evidence; a record assembled at the end is a reconstruction, and you can see the difference in it.

    What it does not entitle us to say

    That our own figures are evidence. The transfer notes are, because somebody outside this firm signs them. Our spreadsheet is us marking our own work.

The one record on a site that an outside party signs

Every other number on this subject is produced by the party it flatters. Waste transfer notes are not — a carrier signs them, a receiving site signs them, and both are registered with somebody who can be asked.

So the figures sit on the notes rather than on this page, and the references sit here instead. Ask for them by job and the set comes over whole, including the loads that went to landfill — which is the part of the set usually left out of the summary.

And the five sentences this page has done without

We are not going to call ourselves green, eco or carbon neutral, because an adjective is not a mechanism and none of those words survives being asked what it does. We do not buy certificates in order to describe a job as neutral — a figure arrived at by purchase is a purchase, and it tells you nothing whatever about how the work was done.

There is no percentage here and no tonnage: those live on the transfer notes, signed by somebody else. And there is no target dated further out than the work we currently hold, because a pledge for a year past the order book is a press release, whereas every window above closes inside a job somebody is already paying for.
