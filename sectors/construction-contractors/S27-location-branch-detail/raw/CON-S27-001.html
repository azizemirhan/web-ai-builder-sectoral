One office and the yard behind it

A contractor’s office is not a shop, and almost nobody reading this is coming here.

The client least of all — your meetings happen on your own site, and the answer you want at seven in the morning is standing on it. So this page is for the three people who genuinely do turn up, and for anybody checking whether this is the office whose mornings reach their job.

Anything further out than the region above is run from another office, and we will tell you which rather than let a job be managed from the wrong end of a motorway.

What is here, and what leaves before the sites start

  • Plant off our own yard

    Loaded and gone the same day it is asked for, which is the only reason a yard is worth what it costs to keep.

  • The fitter, in a van

    A breakdown on a job this office runs is a drive rather than a telephone call to a hire desk.

  • The manager, first thing

    Out before the gangs, which is what makes the morning visit on your site possible at all.

What does not leave here is anything for a site further out — that plant is hired locally, and it arrives from somebody else’s yard on somebody else’s schedule. The yard is emptiest by the time the sites start. If you want somebody standing in it, that is not the hour to come.

The site managers are not. They are on sites, which is the whole point of them, and a page that listed them here would be describing a firm whose people sit in an office. Names are not printed for the same reason they are not printed anywhere else in this catalog: we have not been told them, and a made-up one is worse than an empty field.

The three people who actually come here

  • A delivery

    The wagon comes to the yard gate, not the office door. Somebody in the yard signs for it, and if the yard is empty the office will come out.

    The best hour is the middle of the morning, once the gangs have gone.

  • A supplier or a subcontractor

    Turning up unannounced is not how anybody gets on a list. Send what you do, where you work and your insurances, and somebody will read it.

    If it is a fit, we will ask you in. If it is not, you will get an answer rather than silence.

  • Somebody looking for work

    Come to the yard early, before the gangs go out, and ask for the yard foreman. That is the honest route and it is shorter than any other.

    There is no form here for it, because a form would be slower than the walk.

A client is not on that list, and that is the most useful line on this page. If you have a live job, the person you want is on it or driving to it, and the number you want is your site’s rather than this office’s.

If you are coming anyway

You cannot cross the yard in what you drove in

Boots and a hi-vis, or you will be met in the office instead — which is fine, and is what most people should do anyway. Nobody is going to be pleased about a visitor walking through a load-out in trainers.

Ring first. The person you came to see is very often not here, and the drive is longer than the call.

Getting from the gate to the office

Tell us before you come and we will say honestly what the route is like, including when the answer is that it is not passable that week.

There is no row of tick icons on this page. A yard is not a level floor, and a tick would be a promise about a place nobody has measured.

What is not on this page, and why

  • No opening hours. An empty hours table asserts a weekly schedule, and the honest version is written above: the yard is emptiest by the time the sites start, and the office answers while they are working.
  • No directions, no travel time and no nearest station. Nobody here has measured your journey, and a route written by somebody who has not driven it is worse than no route at all.
  • No claim about how far we reach. There is no such thing as national coverage from one yard, and a job past the region above is run from another office, named when you ask.
