How the site is run

Five questions. Ours are answered. Now ask everybody else.

These are the five that separate a safety programme from a safety policy, and none of them can be answered with a figure. Put the same five to every firm on your tender list, including us, and compare the answers rather than the pages.

  1. 01

    Who on this site can stop the work?

    Anybody on site holds it

    Anybody. Somebody who started this morning, an agency operative, a driver who has come through the gate once. They do not need to find a manager first, and they do not need to be right about the trade they are stopping.

    If a firm answers this with a job title, you have learned something useful about how often it happens.

    What it costs usTime we do not get back, on the morning we can least afford it. That is the price of the authority being real rather than written down.

  2. 02

    What happens to them if they were wrong?

    Anybody on site holds it

    Nothing happens to them. No conversation afterwards, no note anywhere, no quiet reputation for being difficult.

    This is the question that decides whether the first answer was true. A stop that costs the person something is a stop that will not happen the second time, and the second time is the one that matters.

    What it costs usSome stops will be unnecessary, and we pay for those in full. It is a cheaper problem than the alternative.

  3. 03

    Who decides it starts again?

    A named role holds it

    The supervisor and the person who raised it, together. Not the person who stopped it alone, and not whoever is under the most programme pressure that morning — which is the combination that reliably produces a bad restart.

    What it costs usSometimes the rest of the day. The delay is recorded against the programme, never against the person who called it.

  4. 04

    On safety, who does a subcontractor answer to?

    A named role holds it

    Our supervisor — regardless of who they invoice, who introduced them, or how many years they have done it their own way somewhere else.

    We lose subcontractors over this, and we would rather find that out at tender than at first fix.

    What it costs usSubcontractors, and occasionally a cheaper price from one who would not accept it.

  5. 05

    Does any of it change when the job is behind?

    Anybody on site holds it

    No — and this is the only circumstance in which any of the above is worth anything. Every serious incident in this industry happens on a job that is behind.

    We have handed time back on jobs rather than compress a sequence. It is the most expensive sentence on this page and the one we would keep.

    What it costs usProgramme, on the jobs where we could least afford to give it up. That is what makes the answer worth anything.

Do not take these answers from a web page

Every firm you are talking to has a page like this one, and pages are written by people who are not on the site. Ask the five questions standing on a job, of somebody in a hard hat who does not know you are a client.

Come and do it on one of ours. We will not choose which site, and we will not walk you round the part we would have picked.

And the question we get asked sixth

Where are your numbers? Deliberately absent. An incident rate rewards under-reporting — the cheapest way to improve one is to write less down — and a days-without board punishes whoever breaks the streak, who is the person that reported something.

Both make a page look better and a site more dangerous. The certificates are real and they are not here either; they belong where a registration can be checked against a register.
