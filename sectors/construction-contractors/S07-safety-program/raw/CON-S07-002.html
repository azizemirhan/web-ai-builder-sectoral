How the site is run

The questions people ask on the first visit.

In roughly the order they arrive, and answered the way we would answer them standing on the site. A record is an outcome and outcomes can be under-reported. A programme is a set of decisions, and a decision can be checked by asking somebody who works here.

Site area · 3:1 · work happening, real PPE, not posed

  1. Who can stop the work?

    Anybody on site holds it

    Anybody. Somebody who started this morning, an agency operative, a driver who has come through the gate once. They do not need to find a manager first, and they do not need to be right about the trade they are stopping.

    What it costs usTime we do not get back, on a morning when we can least afford it. That is the price of the authority being real rather than written down.

  2. And if they were wrong?

    Anybody on site holds it

    Nothing happens to them. No conversation afterwards, no note anywhere, no quiet reputation for being difficult.

    This is the whole thing, and it is the part most firms get wrong without meaning to. A stop that costs the person something is a stop that does not happen the second time — and the second time is the one that matters.

    What it costs usSome stops will be unnecessary, and we pay for those in full. It is a cheaper problem than the alternative.

  3. Who decides it starts again?

    A named role holds it

    The supervisor and the person who raised it, together. Not the person who stopped it alone, and not whoever is under the most programme pressure that morning — which is the one combination that reliably produces a bad restart.

    What it costs usSometimes the rest of the day. The delay is recorded against the programme, never against the person who called it.

  4. What happens when nothing happened?

    Anybody on site holds it

    The near miss is the one worth having, because it is the only time you learn something at no cost. Whoever saw it says so, it is written down, and it is briefed to everybody the same week rather than filed.

    What it costs usRecording near misses honestly makes our own figures look worse than a firm that does not bother. That is precisely what those figures are worth.

  5. Who does a subcontractor answer to?

    A named role holds it

    On safety, every firm on the site answers to our supervisor — regardless of who they invoice, who introduced them, or how many years they have been doing it their own way somewhere else.

    What it costs usSubcontractors. Some will not work that way, and we would rather discover it at tender than at first fix.

  6. And when the job is behind?

    Anybody on site holds it

    Every serious incident in this industry happens on a job that is behind. None of the above changes when we are behind, which is the only circumstance in which any of it is worth anything.

    What it costs usWe have handed time back on jobs rather than compress a sequence. It is the most expensive line on this page and the one we would keep.

  7. Where are your numbers?

    Deliberately absent

    Deliberately absent. An incident rate rewards under-reporting — the cheapest way to improve one is to write less down. A days-without board punishes whoever breaks the streak, and the person who breaks it is the person who reported something.

    Both make a page look better and a site more dangerous, so a firm printing them is telling you what it optimises. The certificates are real and they are not here either; they belong where a registration can be checked against a register rather than admired on a wall.

    What it costs usA page with no figure on it, next to competitors whose pages have several. We think you can tell the difference, and if you cannot, one visit to either site will.

Everything above can be checked by asking one person on the site, and none of it requires you to take our word. Ask whoever is nearest the gate whether they could stop the job this morning, and whether anything would happen to them afterwards. The answer to that pair of questions is the whole of a safety programme; the rest is administration.
