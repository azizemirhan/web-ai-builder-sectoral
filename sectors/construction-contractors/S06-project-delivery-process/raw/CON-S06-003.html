How a job runs

Two lanes, because a job has two parties in it

Every process diagram in this industry is written entirely in the contractor’s lane. The other one is the same width here, because that is where most of the delay starts.

Site media — the work in progress, wide

What we do

Ours to get right, and ours to carry when it is not.

What you owe before it

Not a complaint. The half nobody prints.

To begin with

The first conversation

Tell you whether this is the kind of work we do, and which of it would be our own people rather than somebody else’s.

If it is not for us, we say so here rather than at the third meeting.

Roughly what, and roughly where.

Who will have the authority to say yes later on — because the answer changes how the rest of this runs.

Fixed after this

Nothing at all. This is the stage where changing your mind is free, and we would rather you spent it.

Before any price

The site walk

We will not price a job we have not walked. A drawing tells you the building; the site tells you the job.

We bring the person who would run it, not only somebody from the office.

Access, at a time when the building is doing what it normally does.

Half an hour of somebody who knows its history — usually the most valuable half hour on the job.

Fixed after this

Still nothing — but what we saw now goes into the price, including an honest line for what we could not see.

Then

The price

A price, and with it the list of what we could not price and why.

Assumptions written where you can argue with them, rather than buried where you will meet them later.

A decision on the exclusions. That list is where the surprises live on every job.

Any information you are holding that we have asked for.

Fixed after this

The scope you accepted. Anything added later is a variation, and it is better to argue about that now than in the middle of the work.

Before we start

Pre-start

Contract, sequence, temporary works, site set-up.

The manager who will run the job is in the meeting, so the plan is made by the person who has to live in it.

The signed contract.

The permissions that are yours to get, and final choices on anything with a long order.

Fixed after this

The sequence. Moving a stage now costs money, because the trades and the plant are booked around it.

While it runs

On site

The work. A written report every week, a valuation every month, and one named person who answers the phone.

Change gets more expensive every week, and the reason is physical. Moving a wall before the frame is a drawing; afterwards it is a demolition.

Decisions when we ask for them rather than when they turn urgent.

This is the largest single cause of delay on a live site, and it is the one thing on this page we cannot do for you.

Fixed after this

Everything you did not change while there was still time to change it cheaply.

At the end

Handover

The building, the drawings, the manuals and the demonstrations, on the day — not a box of paperwork posted later once everyone has forgotten what it was for.

Somebody with the authority to accept it.

Time booked to walk it properly, rather than signed for in a corridor.

Fixed after this

The building. What remains is a defects list, which is a normal part of a job rather than an accusation.

Afterwards

The defects period

The same site manager comes back. Sending somebody who was not there spends your morning explaining the job to a stranger before anything gets fixed.

Tell us. A defect nobody reports is one nobody can fix.

Fixed after this

Nothing. This is the stage where you find out whether the rest of this page was true.

No stage on this page has a length, and none has an empty box where one should go. A duration printed before anybody has walked your site is a guess in a uniform, and a blank marked weeks makes the same promise more quietly. The order is something we can state truthfully; so is where in that order each decision stops being cheap.
