Coordination, and what it is for

It does not stop things going wrong. It moves where you find out.

A coordinated model finds the same problems a site finds. The difference is entirely in the room you are standing in when it turns up — and what fixing it takes there. Which is the only useful way to describe this, so it is the first thing on the page.

  1. Found in the model

    An email

    Before anything is ordered. Somebody moves an object, and the thing that changes is a file.

  2. Found at the drawing review

    A re-issue

    Before fabrication. A revised drawing goes out and somebody has to read it, which is a cost but a small one.

  3. Found on site, in the work

    A variation, and two trades standing

    While it is agreed. The argument about whose it was takes longer than the alteration.

  4. Found after handover

    Opening something that was signed off

    In a building somebody has moved into. This is the one worth paying to avoid.

  • Services against structure

    In the model

    A penetration drawn where it belongs, and a beam designed with it in it rather than around it afterwards.

    On site, if it was missed

    A hole nobody will sign for, and a week of drawings going back and forth while the duct sits on a pallet.

  • Ceiling void depth

    In the model

    A void that holds what is actually going into it, with the fall on the drainage in place rather than assumed.

    On site, if it was missed

    The void is the first thing trimmed when the budget moves and the last thing anybody re-checks afterwards. It is found by the plasterer.

  • Riser and plant space

    In the model

    Room to lift the thing out again, not only to get it in. Maintenance access is a clash the software will not flag, so somebody has to ask.

    On site, if it was missed

    A pump that fits and cannot be replaced. Nobody finds this one during the job; it is found in the building’s fifth year.

  • Builder’s work openings

    In the model

    Every hole agreed and drawn before the pour, with the ones nobody is sure about marked as unresolved rather than left out.

    On site, if it was missed

    Concrete does not have a revision. It is the hardest deadline in the whole exercise, and the reason the model has to be ahead of the programme rather than beside it.

  • Setting out against the survey

    In the model

    The building where it actually is, taken off a survey somebody stood in the room to do.

    On site, if it was missed

    On a refurbishment the building is never where the drawing says it is. A model of an existing structure is only as true as the survey underneath it, and that is the input we will not proceed without.

  • Who owns which object

    In the model

    A schedule saying who models what, to what level of definition, and by when. Not a technology question at all.

    On site, if it was missed

    Two consultants each assuming the other did it, which produces a coordinated-looking model with a hole in the middle of it.

The unglamorous half of this, which is most of it

Most of what goes wrong here is not a clash the software missed. It is somebody modelling to a level of detail nobody agreed, or an object that two parties both thought the other owned, or a file issued after the decision it was meant to inform.

So the useful artefacts are a schedule of who owns what, a level of definition against each, and a date. They are held by a person rather than by a system, and that person is named here rather than described as a department.

And what is not on this page

  • No wireframe
  • No software named
  • No compliance badge
  • No fly-through

A drawing you did not make is a drawing of nothing, so there is no wireframe, no stock plan and no overlay here — and nothing on this page is drawn at an angle, because that is the same picture rebuilt out of rules. Which package we use is the least interesting fact about any of this, and a row of logos would be a wall of badges wearing a different hat. A compliance level is a scope of work rather than an achievement. And a walkthrough sells a building; it does not coordinate one.
