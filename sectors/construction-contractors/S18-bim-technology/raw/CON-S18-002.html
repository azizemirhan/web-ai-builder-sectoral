Coordination, and what it is for

A duct and a beam, and four rooms it could have been found in.

It is the commonest event in this trade, and it is not a story about any particular job — it happens on all of them. What changes is which room somebody is standing in when it turns up, and that is the only thing a coordinated model actually decides.

  1. In the office, with the drawings still open
    What it takes

    An email, and an object moved.

    Somebody with three files open notices that the duct runs through the beam. The engineer adds a penetration; the beam is designed with the hole in it rather than around it afterwards. It takes a morning.

    Nobody writes a case study about this version. It is the entire point of the exercise and it leaves no trace.

  2. At the drawing review, before anything is made
    What it takes

    A revision and a re-issue, and somebody reading it again.

    Nobody caught it in the model, but the sections are put side by side and it shows. A drawing is revised, re-issued and read. The fabricator has not started, so the steel is still a line on a schedule rather than a thing in a yard.

    The cost here is a week of attention and a small amount of goodwill. Still cheap, and still nobody’s fault in a way that has to be argued.

  3. On site, with the steel already up
    What it takes

    A variation, and two trades standing while it is agreed.

    The duct arrives. It does not go. Two trades stand while three parties work out whose drawing was wrong, and the argument about whose it was takes longer than the alteration does.

    A variation is raised. If the programme has float it absorbs it; if it has not, everything behind it moves, and the things behind it are usually the finishes that somebody has already chosen a date for.

    This is where a job’s reputation is made, and it is made before anybody notices it is being made.

  4. After handover, in a building somebody has moved into
    What it takes

    Opening something that was signed off.

    The duct went in, by a route nobody quite signed, through a hole that was made to fit rather than designed to be there. It works. It is found later, by an inspection, or by the beam behaving in a way somebody has to explain.

    The ceiling that has to come down is one the client paid to have made good, in a room that is now in use. This is the one worth paying to avoid, and it is the only one of the four where the cost lands somewhere other than on the people who caused it.

The model does not stop the duct meeting the beam. It decides which of those four rooms you are standing in when it happens.

The six things this is actually done to

Not everything. A model is worth building where a lot of things have to occupy the same space and where getting them wrong is expensive to undo.

  • Services against structure. The duct and the beam, and everything shaped like it.
  • Ceiling void depth. The first thing trimmed when a budget moves and the last thing re-checked after it moves.
  • Riser and plant space. Room to lift the thing out again, not only to get it in — a clash no software flags, so somebody has to ask.
  • Builder’s work openings. Every hole agreed before the pour, because concrete does not have a revision.
  • Setting out against the survey. On a refurbishment the building is never where the drawing says. A model of an existing structure is only as true as the survey underneath it.
  • Who owns which object. A schedule of who models what, to what level, by when. Not a technology question at all.

Most of what goes wrong is not a clash the software missed. It is somebody modelling to a level of detail nobody agreed, an object two parties both believed the other owned, or a file issued after the decision it was supposed to inform.

Which is why the useful artefacts are a schedule, a level against each line of it, and a date — and why they are held by a person rather than by a system. That person is named, not described as a department.

What this page has done without

A wireframe, a stock plan and an overlay — a drawing you did not make is a drawing of nothing, and nothing here is set at an angle either, because that is the same picture rebuilt out of rules. The name of a package, which is the least interesting fact about any of it and would be a wall of badges in a different hat.

A compliance level presented as an achievement, when it is a scope of work. And a walkthrough: a walkthrough sells a building, it does not coordinate one, and the version of this page built around one is selling you the picture rather than the coordination.
