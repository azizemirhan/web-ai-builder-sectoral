Coordination, and what it is for

It does not stop it going wrong. It moves where you find out.

Every problem a coordinated model catches would also have been caught by the building. The difference is the room you are standing in when it turns up, and what fixing it takes there — which is the entire argument, so it gets the whole of the large type and the evidence gets the rest.

The evidence is deliberately dull. A page about this is usually a picture of a model rotating slowly, which is a mood rather than a capability. Underneath is the boring version, which is the true one.

The six things it is actually done to

  1. Services against structure. A penetration designed into the beam rather than cut through it later.
  2. Ceiling void depth. The first thing trimmed when a budget moves, and the last thing anybody re-checks after it moves.
  3. Riser and plant access. Room to lift the thing out again, not only to get it in — a clash the software does not flag, so somebody has to ask.
  4. Builder's work openings. Every hole agreed before the pour, because concrete does not have a revision.
  5. Setting out against the survey. On a refurbishment the building is never where the drawing says it is, and a model of an existing structure is only as true as the survey underneath it.
  6. Who owns which object. A schedule of who models what, to what level, by when. Not a technology question at all.

Most of it is a filing problem wearing a technical hat

What usually goes wrong is not a clash anybody missed. It is somebody modelling to a level of detail nobody agreed, an object two parties each believed the other owned, or a file issued after the decision it was meant to inform.

So the artefacts worth asking about are a schedule, a level against every line of it, and a date — held by a person rather than by a system, and the person is named.

There is no wireframe on this page, no plan, no overlay, and nothing on it is set at an angle — a drawing you did not make is a drawing of nothing, and an angle is the same picture rebuilt out of rules. No package is named, because which one we use is the least interesting fact about any of this, and a row of logos is a wall of badges in a different hat. A compliance level is a scope of work rather than an achievement, so it is not offered as one. There is no walkthrough: a walkthrough sells a building, it does not coordinate one. And there is no claim to a living record after handover — an as-built model that nobody updates is a photograph of a building that has since changed, so who maintains it is settled in writing before it is handed over, and if the answer is nobody we say so.
