The building envelope

A walk up one elevation, stopping where two things meet.

This is how anybody who builds an envelope explains it: you start at the bottom, you go up, and every time two things meet you stop and say whose it is. A list of what is included hides the seams between its own categories. A walk cannot.

  1. At the bottom

    Where the wall meets the ground

    The damp-proof course is set early, on a level somebody surveyed before there was any landscaping. Then the ground goes up. It nearly always goes up: a path is laid, a bed is filled, a threshold is made level for somebody who needs it to be.

    It is the commonest late change on any job, and it is made by a landscaper who has never seen the wall build-up and has no reason to think about it.

    Who owns this joint

    Ours — and we put it in writing the day the level moves, because afterwards nobody remembers agreeing to it.

  2. A storey up

    Where the wall meets a window

    The reveal, the tray over the head, the seal at the frame. Two firms are standing at this one: the window contractor owns the frame and everything sealed to it, and we own the wall it sits in and the tray that keeps water out of the cavity above it.

    The line between those two is a line on a detail, and it is drawn and signed by both of us before either orders anything — because the alternative is drawing it after one of us has already built past it.

    Who owns this joint

    Ours to the frame, theirs from it. Agreed in a drawing, not in a meeting.

  3. Anywhere at all

    Where something goes through it

    Vents, flues, brackets, cables. Each is a hole in a thing whose whole job is to have no holes in it, and each one arrives from a different package on a different week.

    On most jobs this joint has no owner until somebody names one, which is why the second winter is when it gets discussed. We take it, and we say so at the start rather than after.

    Who owns this joint

    Ours, by agreement made at the beginning — whoever made the hole.

  4. At the top

    Where the roof meets the wall

    The parapet, and the tray that stops water running down the inside of the cavity. Two build-ups arrive at one detail, and it is built once, from a scaffold that is already booked to come down.

    Anything found after that scaffold goes is a rope job or another scaffold, so everything that has to be seen is seen while it is still standing, and the sign-off is a condition of striking it.

    Who owns this joint

    Ours, both sides. There is nobody to hand it to and we do not look for one. The covering itself is a retained specialist:

And then we put a hose on the first bay

A water test on a finished building tells you that a finished building leaks. It does not tell you which detail, and by the time anybody asks, the answer is behind a wall that somebody has decorated.

So the test happens at the first bay, before the second one is built. It tests the detail while the detail is still cheap to change, and it is the cheapest thing described on this page.

There is no turnkey version of any of this. A firm claiming there are no interfaces is claiming something no building has, and the interfaces it is not claiming are exactly the ones that leak.
