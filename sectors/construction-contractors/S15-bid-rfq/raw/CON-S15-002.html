Putting work to us

Before you send us anything, here is what we will do with it.

You are probably holding one of three things: a sentence about what you want, a set of drawings, or a tender package. Which one it is decides what you get back from us, and that is worth knowing before you spend an afternoon filling anything in.

There is no calculator on this page and there will not be one. Anything we could work out from a web form would be revised the moment somebody walked your site, and a number that gets revised upward is worse than no number at all — by then it has already done its work of putting us on your list.

Three objects

So: three things you might send, and three different objects that come back

  1. You send a sentence

    You get no number

    A conversation, and a date for us to come and look. That is the honest answer to a sentence, and it is the one most firms will not give you.

  2. You send a design

    You get a range

    With every assumption written beside it where you can argue with it. Useful for a decision. Not useful for a contract.

  3. You send a tender package

    You get a price

    Only this one is a price. The other two are called prices by firms that want to be on your list, and both of them move.

The questions

What we will ask you, and why each of them matters

Where the site is, because it decides whether our site manager is on it every morning — and whether we ought to be bidding at all. Whether the building will be in use, because that is a different job rather than the same job done more carefully; if you cannot tell us, we price it as occupied, which is the expensive assumption.

What stage the design has reached, because that decides which of the three above you get. And what surveys exist — asbestos, ground, structural, drainage — because missing surveys are the single largest cause of a price changing after it has been accepted.

The rule

On that last one we have a rule, and it came from getting it wrong

We once let an asbestos survey stay a provisional item at tender. When it came back it changed the sequence, and everybody spent longer arguing about whose risk it had been than the work itself took.

So we will not price around a missing survey. It has cost us tenders since, to firms happy to price around one, and we would make the same choice again.

The budget

And on your budget, which is the question everybody dodges

We do ask. A scope with no budget attached is a fishing trip for both of us, and pretending otherwise wastes an evening of your time and a week of ours.

What we will not do is simply price to your figure. If the scope costs less than you have allowed, we will say so. If it costs more, we will tell you before we bid rather than after we have won — which is the only version of that conversation that is any use to you.

The walk

Then we come and walk it

Not as a formality and not as a sales visit. A drawing tells you the building; the site tells you the job — the access, the neighbours, the ground, and the thing nobody mentions because they stopped noticing it years ago.

We will not price a job we have not walked, and if what we see says a firm nearer you or a specialist would serve you better, that is what you will get instead of a price. You will have lost a morning rather than a tender.

What is not on this page

  • No instant quote
  • No calculator
  • No figure
  • No response time
  • No win rate

No instant quote, no calculator, no figure of any kind, and no promise about how quickly a price arrives. A response time is a promise about a job nobody has looked at yet, and the usual way of meeting one is to send the first of those three numbers and call it the third.

There is no win rate either, and no count of tenders submitted. Neither would tell you anything about your job.
