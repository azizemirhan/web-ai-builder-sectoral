The plant

Nobody hires a machine. They hire the hole.

A fleet list is a car advert with the prices removed — a row of clean machines with their weights printed underneath. What follows is the argument for organising the same information the other way round, by the site conditions the machines exist to answer.

The specification is the wrong document

A specification tells you what a machine can do somewhere. It is written by the manufacturer, it is true, and it is almost never the thing standing between you and a finished dig.

What stands between you and a finished dig is your gate. Then your ground, then your neighbours, then the hours you are allowed to make noise in. None of those appear on a specification sheet, and all of them decide which machine is any use to you.

The gate comes first

Ask any groundworker what they want to know about a job and they will ask about access before they ask about volume. A back garden reached through a side return is a different trade from the same hole on an open plot, and the difference is not skill; it is what fits through the gap.

So the fleet is held to answer that ladder rather than to look impressive on a yard: plant that goes through a domestic gate, plant that goes through a site gate, and plant that arrives on a low-loader and needs somewhere to stand when it gets there.

And the tier above thatAnything needing a crane and a road closure is a specialist, a permit and a programme of its own. We say so at tender rather than discovering it at the frame.

Then the ground, which is rarely what the survey said

A basement below, a podium slab, a vault under the pavement. The machine is not usually the problem; where it is allowed to stand is. Mats, a lighter machine working further out, and a stance agreed by the temporary works engineer rather than by whoever is on site first.

This is the part of a plant page that reads as dull and decides how a job actually goes. Nobody photographs it.

The operator is the capability

A machine is as good as the person in it, and the person is not on the specification either. Ours are ticketed and on our own books, and we put the same operator on a job from start to finish wherever the programme lets us, because somebody who has learned your site is worth more than a better machine with a stranger in it.

And when it stops

Owned plant means our fitter and our cost. That is not a boast, it is a description of who waits: a breakdown on ours is a bad morning, and a breakdown on a hired machine is a queue at somebody else’s yard with your programme in it.

Some of the fleet is deliberately hired anyway, because plant standing idle half the year ends up in somebody’s preliminaries and that somebody is the client. Which of it is which is set out on the capabilities page, and the honest half of that list is the interesting half.

What the quiet answer is

In an occupied building the constraint is the clock rather than the decibel. We bring battery plant where it exists and hydraulic rather than percussive breaking where it does not — and much of this fleet has no quiet equivalent yet, so the real answer is sequencing the noisy work rather than claiming a silent version of it exists.

There are no makes, models, weights, reaches or tonnages on this page, and no fleet value or hours run. A specification tells you what a machine can do somewhere; whether it can get down your access, stand on your ground and work inside your hours is a different question and the only one worth answering here. The picture is of a machine working with somebody in it, because a clean machine on a forecourt is an advertisement for plant rather than evidence of work.
