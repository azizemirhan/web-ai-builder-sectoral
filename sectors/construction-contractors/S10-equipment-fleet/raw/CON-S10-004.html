The access question is the one thing on a page like this that can honestly be answered before anybody visits — and it is the question that stops jobs before they start. Three photographs, and you will know what can get in.

  • Photograph one

    The way in

    Stand in the street and photograph the opening, with something ordinary next to it — a wheelie bin, a car, a front door — so there is something to read the width against.

    What it answersWhich access tier you are in, and therefore whether this is micro plant through a gate, ordinary plant through a site entrance, or something arriving on a low-loader.

  • Photograph two

    Where a machine would have to stand

    The ground between the opening and the work. Grass, slabs, a driveway, a manhole cover, anything that looks like it might be over a cellar.

    What it answersWhether we can track straight to it, whether it needs mats, or whether we are working at reach from a stance somebody has to agree first.

  • Photograph three

    The route out

    Where a lorry would sit, and what is opposite it. A school gate, a bus stop, a corner nobody can see round.

    What it answersWhether muck away is straightforward or whether it needs a wash point, sheeting and a route agreed with you before the first load.

Plant media — a machine tracking in through a restricted access, operator in it

You will get a tier back, not a quotation

Which of the four access tiers your site is in, what fits, and what does not. If the honest answer is that it needs a crane and a road closure, you will get that too — and the name of the kind of firm to call instead of us.

It costs you the time it takes to walk to the gate, and it costs us a reply. Neither of us has committed to anything.

What a photograph will not tell us

What is under the ground. No picture settles that, and we will not pretend one did — a survey is a hypothesis and the dig is the test.

It also will not tell us what the neighbours think, or which hours you are allowed to make noise in. Those come out of a walk, and we will not price a job we have not walked.

A specification tells you what a machine can do somewhere. Whether it fits your opening, stands on your ground and works in your hours is a different question, and it is the only one this page tries to answer.

The answer you get back is a tier rather than a measurement, for the same reason. The picture above is of a machine working with somebody in it, because a clean machine on a forecourt is an advertisement rather than evidence.
