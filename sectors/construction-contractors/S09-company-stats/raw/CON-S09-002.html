The firm, in figures

The number is the part nobody can evaluate.

Which is why, on this page, it is smaller than the sentence about it. There are four figures here and four different kinds of figure — a fixed point, a running total, a snapshot and a single extreme — and the usual row of four identical boxes is what stops a reader noticing the difference.

A fixed point

Year founded

Year the firm was founded—
Under this name since
Year the current trading name was adopted—

A date is not a score

It is the one figure in a stats band that cannot be improved by doing good work, and it does not grow because a firm is any better this year than last. It grows because time passed.

The second line is the one worth reading. Firms change names after a bad job, after an insolvency, after a sale; a long history and a short trading name are a combination worth asking about, and we would rather print both than the flattering one.

A running total

Projects completed

Number of projects completed—
Counted since
Year the count starts from—
A project means
Definition of a counted project—

A total with no start date can be any number you like

Counted from the founding year it means one thing; counted from last spring it means another; and the industry convention is to leave the question open and let the reader assume whichever is more impressive.

The definition matters as much as the count. A firm counting every call-out will always beat one counting contracts, and neither number tells you anything until you know which of the two you are looking at.

A snapshot

People employed

Number of people employed—
As at
Date the headcount was taken—
On our own books
Number directly employed—

True on the day it was counted, and different next month

Headcount in this industry moves with the order book. A figure printed without a date is being presented as permanent when it is provisional, and the provisional version is the honest one.

The split is the part that tells you something. How many of those people are on our own books rather than on somebody else’s is a different question from how many are on site, and the capabilities page is where we answer it properly.

A single extreme

Largest project by value

Value of the largest project—
That one job, completed
Year the largest project completed—

The largest project we have done is not the size of project we usually do

This is the figure most designed to be misread, and the misreading runs in the direction that costs a client something: it is the one that makes somebody with an ordinary job decide they are too small to bother us with.

They are not, and it is one job. An extreme is not an average, a typical value or a floor, and printing it beside three other numbers in an identical box invites all three of those readings at once.

And the numbers that are not here at all

No happy clients — that is a client list with a compliment attached, and nobody ran the survey. No satisfaction or on-time percentage, because both assert a measurement system and the definition of on time belongs to whoever reports it. No awards: a body we chose, making a judgement we paid to enter.

No safety figure, for a harder reason set out on the safety page. If a figure is countable and it is not one of the four above, it is a claim rather than a count.
