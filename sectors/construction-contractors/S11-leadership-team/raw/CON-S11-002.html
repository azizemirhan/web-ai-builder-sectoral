The people

In the order you will actually meet them.

A team page is usually a gallery, ordered by seniority, and it answers a question nobody asked. The question a client has is chronological: who is in front of me now, and who will be in front of me in month three? Those are rarely the same person, and the gap between them is the most common complaint about contractors in this industry.

At the tender meeting

Managing director

Decideschanges the contract, turns work down. Nothing on your site day to day.

You will meet the managing director, because that is who comes to tender interviews in a firm of this size, and because turning work down is their decision rather than anybody else’s.

You should assume you will not see them again until handover. Most contractors leave that unsaid and let the impression stand; it is a pleasant impression and it is not true. A firm where the managing director is making site decisions in month three is a firm where either the site manager has no authority or the director cannot leave it alone, and neither of those helps the job you are paying for.

Before anything is signed

Contracts manager

Decidesagrees a variation, moves resource between sites. This is the escalation.

The contracts manager comes on the walk, because the person who will carry your job should see it before anybody prices it. This is the escalation for the whole of the work: when something cannot be fixed inside the week, it becomes their problem rather than the director’s.

The number you are given at pre-start is theirs, not a switchboard, and they carry your job on a list with a small number of others rather than a long one.

At pre-start

Planner

Decidesrewrites the programme. Nobody else does.

Two more people appear, and one of them writes the thing everything else runs on. The planner walks the site before writing the programme and again whenever it changes — a programme written from a desk is a spreadsheet, and everyone on site knows within a fortnight which of the two they have been given.

The commercial manager appears here too, and the useful fact about them is that they are the same person who priced it. Handing a job to a surveyor who did not price it is where most variation arguments begin.

From the first morning

Site manager

Decidesstops the work, orders what the day needs, moves the sequence within the week.

Here is the swap, and it is the whole reason this page is written as a sequence. From the day the work starts, the person you deal with is the site manager — every morning, about everything — and they will make more decisions about your building in a week than the director will make all year.

They run one site rather than three. Where we cannot resource that, we decline the job, which is a sentence worth checking against how many jobs we are running when you ask.

Beside them is the foreman, who briefs the gang every morning before anybody picks anything up, and who has the same authority to stop the work as everybody else on the site. The foreman decides: runs the gang, stops the work, calls the site manager rather than working round it.

And at handover

Commercial manager

Decidesagrees the valuation, prices a change.

The director comes back, which is the honest shape of it: present at the two moments where the decision is theirs, and absent in between because the decisions in between are not.

The same site manager returns through the defects period as well. Sending somebody who was not there spends your morning explaining the job to a stranger before anything gets fixed.

It decides how many jobs we can run properly at once, which is why it is a reason we decline work rather than a reason we win it.

What is not written here

No qualifications, no memberships, no years in the trade, no career histories. They are true of everybody on this page and they distinguish nobody — every competitor’s team page carries the same list.

What is here instead is who you will see, when, and what they can decide without asking. All of it can be checked by asking the person it is written under.
