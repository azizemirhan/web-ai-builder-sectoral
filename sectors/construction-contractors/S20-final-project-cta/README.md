# Section Role

The last thing this firm says before a reader decides — and the things it is prepared to be held to
if they do.

# The Section Arrives With Almost Nothing Left

By the time a reader reaches it, four rules and three sections have taken everything a closing band
normally contains:

| Taken by | What it removes |
| --- | --- |
| Anti-pattern seven | **The countdown, the limited slots, the filling order book.** *Nobody buys a building on urgency* |
| `S15` Bid RFQ | The submission, and any promise of a price |
| `S19` Contact | The routing, the numbers, and who answers what |
| The `S06` rule | Any promise about how fast we reply |
| `S15-004` | The sector's only form |

What is left is the interesting part.

# The Governing Constraint — A Close Is A Commitment, Not A Button

    A closing section is not a button. It is the last thing we say before you decide,
    so it should be the thing we are prepared to be held to.

Every closing band in this industry asks the reader to do something. **This one states what the firm
will do**, in terms specific enough to be broken visibly.

# The Five Commitments

| We will | And here is how you would know if we broke it |
| --- | --- |
| **Come and look at it.** No number before somebody has stood on the ground | A quotation arrives by email from somebody who has never seen the site |
| **Send the person who would run it**, not somebody whose job is winning work | You meet somebody once at the start and never again |
| **Say at the walk if we are the wrong firm**, and name somebody nearer or better suited | Nobody ever says no, and you find out from a price that reads strangely |
| **Say at the walk if your fixed start is not achievable** — rather than in week six | A programme that only becomes impossible after the contract is signed |
| **Not chase you.** One call after the visit; after that it is your move | A sequence of emails written by somebody who was not at the visit |

# The Device — Every Commitment Carries The Question That Tests It

A promise a reader cannot check is decoration. So each commitment is published with **the question to
ask us at the walk**, which is the part that makes it cost something:

> *Who is coming, and have they seen it?* · *Will you be on this job?* · *Is this the sort of job you
> turn down?* · *What would have to be true for this start to hold?* · *What happens if I do not
> reply?*

That is `S18`'s consequence device turned around: instead of naming what the firm will not claim,
this section hands the reader the test.

# What To Have Ready, So The Morning Is Not Wasted

- **Whatever drawings exist, even if they are out of date.** Out of date is information; nothing is
  not.
- **Who decides**, and what that person can sign without asking somebody else.
- **What fixes your start** — a term, a lease, a season.
- **Anything another firm has already told you**, so we can disagree with it in front of you rather
  than behind them.

# What This Section Will Not Do

- **No countdown, no limited slots, no *our order book is filling*.** Anti-pattern seven, including
  its polite version. Capacity is stated only as something that can be bad news: *if your start is
  fixed and we cannot meet it, you will hear that at the walk.*
- **No free quote, no *no obligation*, no price.** `S15` owns that, and the answer there is that a
  price is a function of information.
- **No response-time promise.** The `S06` rule.
- **No numbers or routing.** `S19` owns those; this section is a commitment, not a directory.
- **No *let us build something together*.** A sentence with nothing in it that could be broken.

# The Fewest Reserved Fields In The Sector

`S19` is almost entirely fields, and this section is almost none: **a commitment does not need a
number attached to it.** Two or three reserved fields per study at most — the person who would come,
and where to ask for the walk. Everything else on these pages is authored and answerable for.
