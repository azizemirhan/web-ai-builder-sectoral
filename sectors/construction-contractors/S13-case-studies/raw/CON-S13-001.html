Particular jobs

What we found. What we chose. What it cost us.

Nobody who ran a job remembers an outcome. They remember three or four moments where something was found and somebody had to choose. So that is what is here — including, on each one, a decision we got wrong.

What we found

The slab was thinner than the record drawings showed, in the one bay where the new plant had to sit.

What we chose

Redesigned the fixings rather than re-pouring, with the temporary works engineer on site the same day rather than the following week.

What it cost us

The float we were holding on the fit-out sequence. Ours, not the client’s.

What we found

The agreed noisy hours did not survive contact with the ward below, whose day started earlier than the estates team had told us.

What we chose

Re-sequenced the breaking into the afternoons and gave the mornings to the trades that make no noise, rather than asking the ward to move.

What it cost us

Two trades on site where one would have done. We absorbed the difference.

What we found

A services route on the drawings ran straight through a structural opening that had been filled at some point nobody had recorded.

What we chose

Stopped that trade and raised it as a design query the same morning rather than cutting and asking afterwards.

What it cost us

The trade went to another job while we waited, and we took them back when the answer came.

The one we got wrong

We let the asbestos survey stay a provisional item at tender instead of insisting on it first. When it came back it changed the sequence, and everybody then spent longer arguing about whose risk it had been than the work itself took.

What changedWe now decline to price this kind of refurbishment without the survey in hand. It has cost us two tenders since, both to firms who were happy to price around it.

What we found

The ground came up softer than the site investigation suggested across about a third of the footprint.

What we chose

Self-delivered the remedial groundworks rather than sublet them, because we would rather be the ones standing in the hole when it disagrees with the survey.

What it cost us

Our own gang moved off another job to do it. That job started later, and we told its client why.

What we found

The steel delivery was going to land in the same week as the school’s open evening, on the only access route.

What we chose

Moved the erection sequence rather than the school’s evening, and agreed the gate times with the site staff rather than the estates office.

What it cost us

The sequence rather than the price. We did not ask for it back.

What we found

A long-order component was going to arrive after the date we needed it, and the supplier told us late.

What we chose

Told the client the same day rather than at the next progress meeting, with two options and what each of them meant for the term date.

What it cost us

An uncomfortable phone call earlier than we would have liked, which is the cheapest thing on this page.

The one we got wrong

We took the client’s word that the long-order items had been signed off, and did not check the order had actually been placed. It had not. We found out at the point where finding out was most expensive.

What changedLong-order items are now confirmed by order number at pre-start and checked again by the planner, and that check is a line on our own programme rather than an assumption about somebody else’s.

What is not at the end of these

  • On time
  • On budget
  • A delighted client
  • Any percentage
  • Any value or duration

That paragraph appears on the case studies of firms that failed, because it is written afterwards by somebody who was not on the job. We would rather show you the decisions, which are checkable by asking the client about any one of them.

Cost is described rather than counted, for the same reason. Out of our float rather than yours is something a client will either confirm or contradict; a figure is something you would have to take on trust.
